Example 4.A.13Block slipping on a rotating arm

polar componentsstatic frictionvertical plane

Problem

Arm CA turns in a vertical plane about the fixed pin C at a constant rate $\omega$. When CA is horizontal, block P (mass $m$) rests on the arm at a distance $r$ from C and does not move relative to the arm. P starts to slip relative to CA when the arm reaches the angle $\theta_{slip}$.

The data are $\omega = 2$ rad/s, $r = 3$ ft and $\theta_{slip} = 53.13°$.

Find the coefficient of static friction $\mu_s$ between P and CA.

InteractiveThe arm turns at a constant rate until friction can no longer hold P

The forces on P are drawn to scale. The graph shows the friction needed to hold P, divided by the normal force. P slips when this ratio reaches $\pm\mu_s$. After slipping, the block slides toward C with the kinetic coefficient $\mu_k$, which the book does not give, so it has its own slider.

Solution

Every solution follows the four-step method: free-body diagrams, kinetics equations, kinematics, solve. Try the problem first, and then open one step at a time to check your work.

Step 1 · Draw the free-body diagram

Isolate P while it is still held by friction. Three forces act on it, the weight $mg$, the normal force $N$ perpendicular to the arm, and the friction force $f$ along the arm. Use polar coordinates centered at C, with $\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ along the arm and $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ perpendicular to it. We take $f$ positive in the $+\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ direction.

The block is acted on by its weight $mg$, the normal force $N$ perpendicular to the arm, and the friction force $f$ along the arm. We draw $f$ pointing away from C, in the $+\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ direction, and let its sign decide its true direction.

Step 2 · Write the kinetics equations

The weight has components $-mg\sin\theta$ along $\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ and $-mg\cos\theta$ along $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$. Newton's second law gives

$$\sum F_r = m a_r: \quad f - mg\sin\theta = m a_r, \qquad \sum F_\theta = m a_\theta: \quad N - mg\cos\theta = m a_\theta$$
Step 3 · Write the kinematics equations

Until it slips, P stays at $r = 3$ ft, so $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$. The arm turns at a constant rate, so $\dot\omega = 0$. Then

$$a_r = \ddot r - r\omega^2 = -r\omega^2 = -12\ \text{ft/s}^2, \qquad a_\theta = r\dot\omega + 2\dot r\omega = 0$$
Step 4 · Solve

The kinetics equations give the friction force needed to hold P and the normal force,

$$f = m\,(g\sin\theta - r\omega^2), \qquad N = mg\cos\theta$$

P is about to slip when $|f| = \mu_s N$. At $\theta_{slip} = 53.13°$, $\sin\theta = 0.8$ and $\cos\theta = 0.6$, so

$$\mu_s = \frac{g\sin\theta - r\omega^2}{g\cos\theta} = \frac{32.2(0.8) - 12}{32.2(0.6)} = \frac{13.76}{19.32} = 0.712$$

The value of $f$ is positive, so friction acts away from C and P slips down the arm toward C. When the arm is horizontal, $f/N = -12/32.2 = -0.373$, which is well within $\pm 0.712$, so P could not have slipped earlier.

Answer

$\mu_s = 0.712$. At the slip angle friction acts up the arm, away from C, and P starts to slide toward C.

Explore

  • Lower $\mu_s$. How does the slip angle change, and does P ever slip outward instead of inward?
  • Increase $\omega$ to 3 rad/s. Why does P now need friction pointing toward C when the arm is horizontal?
