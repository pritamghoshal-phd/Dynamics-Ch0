Chapter 4 · Particle Kinetics

Chapters 1–3 described how things move. Kinetics connects motion to its cause: forces. This chapter treats every body as a particle (all its mass at one point) and develops four tools that all grow out of Newton's second law. Each tool comes with animations you can push around.

The four-step method for kinetics

  1. Free-body diagrams. Decide which body (or group of bodies) to isolate and draw every force on it. Draw your coordinate directions and unit vectors on the diagram too.
  2. Kinetics equations. Pick the principle that suits the question (Newton's second law, work–energy, linear impulse–momentum or angular impulse–momentum) and write its equations from your FBDs.
  3. Kinematics. Express the accelerations, velocities or displacements that appear in Step 2 using the kinematics of Chapters 1–3 (path, polar or Cartesian components, constraints).
  4. Solve. Count unknowns and equations. Too few equations means you need another FBD or more kinematics. Then solve.

Which tool when?

All four principles are Newton's second law in different clothing: integrate it along the path and you get work–energy; integrate it over time and you get impulse–momentum; take its moment about a point and you get angular momentum. The question usually tells you which one is fastest:

The question involves…Reach for…
forces and accelerations at one instantNewton's second law, $\sum\mathbf F = m\mathbf a$
speeds at two positionswork–energy, $T_1 + V_1 + U^{nc}_{1\to2} = T_2 + V_2$
velocities at two times, or a collisionlinear impulse–momentum, $m\mathbf v_1 + \int\mathbf F\,dt = m\mathbf v_2$
rotation about a point, central forces, "pulled inward"angular impulse–momentum, $\mathbf H_{O1} + \int\mathbf M_O\,dt = \mathbf H_{O2}$
