DAngular impulse and momentum

The angular momentum of a particle about a fixed point O is the moment of its linear momentum:

$$\mathbf H_O = \mathbf r_{P/O}\times m\mathbf v$$

Taking the moment of Newton's law about O and noting that $\dot{\mathbf r}\times m\mathbf v = \mathbf v\times m\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ gives

$$\sum\mathbf M_O = \dot{\mathbf H}_O \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf H_{O1} + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum\mathbf M_O\,dt = \mathbf H_{O2}$$

In plane motion, $\mathbf H_O$ points out of the plane. In polar coordinates centered at O, only the transverse velocity contributes: $H_O = m\,r\,v_\theta = m r^2\dot\theta$. The radial velocity is parallel to $\mathbf r$, so its cross product with $\mathbf r$ is zero.

Central forces: angular momentum is conserved

If the resultant force on a particle always points through a fixed point O (a cord through a hole, a planet's gravity, a spring anchored at O), it has no moment about O. Then $\mathbf H_O$ is constant:

$$r_1\,v_{\theta 1} = r_2\,v_{\theta 2}$$

Pull the particle in and it must speed up. Note that energy is not conserved here in general: the pull on the cord does work.

ConceptPull a cord through a hole

A puck on a smooth table is tied to a cord through a hole at O. As you draw the cord in, r shrinks and the transverse speed grows so that m r v_θ stays fixed. The cord force points through O, so it can change the speed but not H_O.

Equal areas in equal times

For any central force, the area swept by the radius line per unit time is $\tfrac12 r^2\dot\theta = H_O/2m$, a constant. This is Kepler's second law of planetary motion, and it holds for every central force, not only gravity.

Systems of particles

For a system, internal forces come in equal, opposite and collinear pairs, so their moments about any point cancel. The total angular momentum about a fixed point changes only through the moments of the external forces:

$$\sum_j \mathbf H_{O,j\,1} + \int\sum\mathbf M_{O,\text{ext}}\,dt = \sum_j\mathbf H_{O,j\,2}$$

When a system spins freely about a fixed axis with no external moment about it (a skater, a rotating arm with sliding beads), its total angular momentum about that axis is conserved, even while parts move in or out.

Angular momentum in the wild

A figure skater spinning with arms out pulls them in and spins much faster. No external moment acts about the vertical axis (the ice is nearly frictionless), so the total angular momentum stays fixed, and bringing mass closer to the axis forces the rotation rate up, exactly like the puck on the cord.
