Example 4.D.1Particle sliding out along a spinning arm

angular momentumconservationpolar components

Problem

Particle P, which weighs 2 lb, can slide on a smooth, lightweight horizontal arm that turns freely about a vertical axis through O. Initially P is at rest relative to the arm, at a distance $R_1 = 3$ in from the axis, while the arm turns at $\omega_1 = 20$ rad/s. P is then free to slide.

Find the angular speed of the arm when P has moved outward to $R_2 = 24$ in from the axis of the shaft.

InteractiveTop view of the arm while P slides outward

Because the arm is lightweight and smooth, it cannot push on P, so P moves along a straight line at constant speed (dashed green line). The arm simply follows P, and its rate drops as $1/R^2$.

Solution

Every solution follows the four-step method: free-body diagrams, kinetics equations, kinematics, solve. Try the problem first, and then open one step at a time to check your work.

Step 1 · Draw the free-body diagrams

First take the shaft, the arm and P together. The bearing forces act on the axis of rotation, and the weight $W$ of P is parallel to that axis. So none of the external forces has a moment about the axis.

Then look at P alone in a top view. The smooth arm can only push on P across the arm, with a force $N$. The arm itself has negligible mass and nothing drives it, so the sum of the moments on the arm about the axis must be zero. The only such moment is $R N$, which means $N = 0$.

Left: the bearing forces act on the axis and the weight $W$ is parallel to it, so no external force has a moment about the axis. Right: the smooth arm can push on P only across the arm. Because the arm is lightweight and turns freely, the moment $R N$ of this force about the axis must be zero, so $N = 0$ while P slides.

Step 2 · Write the kinetics equation (angular impulse and momentum)

No external force has a moment about the vertical axis, so the angular momentum of the system about that axis is conserved. The arm has negligible mass, so all of this angular momentum belongs to P. Only the transverse velocity component $v_\theta$ of P contributes, because the radial component points at the axis.

$$H_{O1} = H_{O2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m R_1 v_{\theta 1} = m R_2 v_{\theta 2}$$
Step 3 · Write the kinematics equations

P stays on the arm, so its transverse velocity component equals the speed of the point of the arm that it occupies.

$$v_{\theta} = R\,\omega$$

Substituting this into the conservation equation gives $R_1^2\,\omega_1 = R_2^2\,\omega_2$. The mass of P cancels, so its weight is not needed.

Step 4 · Solve $$\omega_2 = \omega_1\left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)^2 = 20\left(\frac{3}{24}\right)^2 = 0.3125\ \text{rad/s}$$

A useful check comes from the second free-body diagram. With $N = 0$ no horizontal force acts on P, so P moves in a straight line at its initial speed $R_1\omega_1 = 60$ in/s. It reaches $R = 24$ in after $\sqrt{24^2 - 3^2}/60 = 0.397$ s, when the arm has turned through $82.8°$. At that point $v_\theta = 3(60)/24 = 7.5$ in/s, which again gives $\omega_2 = 7.5/24 = 0.3125$ rad/s.

Answer

$\omega_2 = 0.3125$ rad/s, which is $1/64$ of the initial rate.

Explore

  • Change the initial distance $R_1$. Why does the final rate depend so strongly on it?
  • Watch the velocity arrows. The speed of P never changes, but its direction relative to the arm does. Where does the transverse component go?
  • Suppose a motor kept the arm turning at 20 rad/s. Would the angular momentum of P still be conserved? Why not?
