ANewton's laws

For a particle of mass $m$ acted on by forces $\mathbf F_1, \mathbf F_2, \dots$, Newton's second law says the resultant force equals mass times acceleration:

$$\sum \mathbf F = m\,\mathbf a$$

This is one vector equation, so in the plane it gives two scalar equations. You may write them in whichever components make the acceleration easiest, using the kinematics of Chapter 1:

ComponentsEquationsGood for
Cartesian$\sum F_x = m\ddot x,\quad \sum F_y = m\ddot y$straight lines, projectiles
Path (normal–tangential)$\sum F_t = m\dot v,\quad \sum F_n = m\dfrac{v^2}{\rho}$known curved paths, speeds
Polar$\sum F_r = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2),\quad \sum F_\theta = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)$rotating arms, slots, cords through holes

The force side comes from the FBD; the acceleration side comes from kinematics. Keeping the two sides separate is the whole discipline of the four-step method.

There is no "centrifugal force"

When a body moves on a curve, the normal equation $\sum F_n = m v^2/\rho$ says the forces must pull inward to bend the path. Some people move $mv^2/\rho$ to the left side and call it an outward "centrifugal force" so the problem looks like statics. In this course we do not: an FBD shows only real forces exerted by real things (cords, surfaces, gravity, springs). The term $mv^2/\rho$ is part of $m\mathbf a$, not a force.

While the string is attached, the only horizontal force is the tension pulling toward O. Nothing pushes outward. Cut the string and the ball does not fly outward along the radius: it continues straight along the tangent, exactly as Newton's first law says.

Normal forces change on curved paths

On a road with hills and dips, the normal force is not $mg$. At the crest of a hill (center of curvature below), $mg - N = mv^2/\rho$, so $N = mg - mv^2/\rho$: the car feels lighter, and if $v^2 > g\rho$ the road would have to pull the car down, which it cannot, so the car leaves the road. In a dip, $N = mg + mv^2/\rho$ and the car feels heavier.

Straight-line motion: integrating F = ma

When a particle moves along a line, Newton's law is a scalar equation $F = m\,dv/dt$. Depending on what the force depends on, separate variables just as in Chapter 0:

  • $F(t)$: $\;m\,dv = F\,dt \Rightarrow m(v_2 - v_1) = \int F\,dt$
  • $F(s)$: $\;m v\,dv = F\,ds \Rightarrow \tfrac12 m(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \int F\,ds$
  • $F(v)$: $\;dt = m\,dv/F(v)$, or $ds = m v\,dv/F(v)$

These two integrals are the seeds of the next two sections: impulse–momentum and work–energy.

Systems of particles

Write Newton's law for each particle in a system and add them up. The forces particles exert on each other come in equal and opposite pairs (Newton's third law) and cancel, leaving

$$\sum \mathbf F_{\text{ext}} = M\,\mathbf a_G, \qquad M = \sum m_j,\quad \mathbf r_G = \frac{1}{M}\sum m_j\mathbf r_j$$

The center of mass G moves as if all the mass were concentrated there and all the external forces acted on it. Internal forces can shake the parts around, but they cannot move G.

ConceptInternal forces cannot move the center of mass
ABcenter of mass GG predicted by F = (m_A + m_B) a_G

A force F pulls only on A; a spring links A and B. A and B jiggle back and forth, but G (thick purple) follows the smooth parabola of a single particle of mass m_A + m_B pushed by F.

The four-step method for kinetics

  1. Free-body diagrams. Decide which body (or group of bodies) to isolate and draw every force on it. Draw your coordinate directions and unit vectors on the diagram too.
  2. Kinetics equations. Pick the principle that suits the question (Newton's second law, work–energy, linear impulse–momentum or angular impulse–momentum) and write its equations from your FBDs.
  3. Kinematics. Express the accelerations, velocities or displacements that appear in Step 2 using the kinematics of Chapters 1–3 (path, polar or Cartesian components, constraints).
  4. Solve. Count unknowns and equations. Too few equations means you need another FBD or more kinematics. Then solve.

🎢
Kinetics in the wild

Roller-coaster designers use $N = mg \pm mv^2/\rho$ all the time. The "airtime" hills that lift you out of your seat are crests shaped so that N briefly drops to nearly zero. Loops use a teardrop (clothoid) shape so the curvature is tight at the top, where the train is slow, and gentle at the bottom, where it is fast, keeping N comfortable everywhere.
