CLinear impulse and momentum

Integrate Newton's law over time instead of distance:

$$m\mathbf v_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum\mathbf F\,dt = m\mathbf v_2$$

$m\mathbf v$ is the linear momentum and $\int\mathbf F\,dt$ is the impulse of the forces. This is a vector equation (two scalar equations in the plane). It is the natural tool when the question is about velocities at two times, or when forces act over a short time that you do not know in detail, as in a collision.

The impulse is the area under the force–time curve. The same change in momentum can come from a large force over a short time or a small force over a long time, which is the whole idea behind airbags, helmets and bent knees.

Conservation of momentum

For a system of particles, the internal forces cancel in pairs, so

$$\sum m_j\mathbf v_{j1} + \int\sum\mathbf F_{\text{ext}}\,dt = \sum m_j\mathbf v_{j2}$$

If the external impulse is zero in some direction (for example, a smooth floor during a short collision), the total momentum in that direction is conserved. Forces like gravity and spring forces are finite, so over a very short impact their impulse is negligible: during a collision, only the large impact forces matter.

Central impact and the coefficient of restitution

When two particles collide along the line joining their centers (the line of impact), momentum conservation gives one equation for two unknown final velocities. The second comes from how bouncy the collision is, measured by the coefficient of restitution:

$$e = \frac{v_{B2} - v_{A2}}{v_{A1} - v_{B1}} \qquad (0 \le e \le 1)$$

the speed of separation over the speed of approach. $e = 1$ is perfectly elastic (kinetic energy is kept); $e = 0$ is perfectly plastic (the particles stick together). Kinetic energy is not conserved in general, so do not use work–energy across an impact.

Momentum before and after is always the same. Kinetic energy is the same only when e = 1. With equal masses and e = 1, the blocks swap velocities, like Newton's cradle.

Oblique impact

If the velocities are not along the line of impact, split them into a normal component (along the line of impact) and a tangential component (in the plane of contact). For smooth surfaces there is no tangential impact force, so each particle keeps its own tangential velocity. Along the normal, use momentum conservation and the restitution equation as above.

ConceptA puck bounces off a smooth wall

The component along the wall is untouched; the normal component reverses and shrinks by the factor e. So the puck always leaves at a flatter angle than it arrived unless e = 1.

Momentum in the wild

A good pool player uses central impact constantly. A head-on hit with the cue ball (nearly equal masses, e close to 1) stops the cue ball dead and sends the object ball off at its speed. Hit off-center and the oblique-impact rule takes over: the object ball leaves along the line joining the centers, and the cue ball keeps its tangential velocity, so the two leave at roughly 90° to each other.
