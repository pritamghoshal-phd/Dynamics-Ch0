CScalar kinematics

Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. For a point P moving along a line, its position is a single number $s(t)$. Its velocity and acceleration are

$$v = \frac{ds}{dt}=\dot s, \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}=\ddot s$$

Geometrically, velocity is the slope of the $s$–$t$ curve and acceleration is the slope of the $v$–$t$ curve. Going the other way, integration undoes differentiation, so areas under the curves give changes:

$$v(t) = v(0) + \int_0^t a\,dt, \qquad s(t) = s(0) + \int_0^t v\,dt$$
ConceptSlopes and areas: s, v and a together
Motion:

The dashed orange lines are tangents: the slope of the s curve is the current v, and the slope of the v curve is the current a. The shaded areas are integrals: the area under a (so far) is the change in v, and the area under v is the change in s.

When acceleration depends on position: the chain rule

Sometimes we know the velocity or the acceleration as a function of position, not time. A spring force, for example, depends on how far the spring is stretched. Then the chain rule lets us avoid time altogether:

$$a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{ds}\,\frac{ds}{dt} = v\,\frac{dv}{ds}$$

In words: the acceleration is the speed times the slope of the speed-vs-position curve. Moving fast up a steep part of that curve means a large acceleration.

Concepta = v dv/ds, checked against the motion
Speed profile:

Point P moves with the speed v(s) shown on the left. The dashed tangent has slope dv/ds. Multiply by v and you get the acceleration on the right. The last statistic measures the acceleration directly from the motion (change in speed over a short time); the two agree.

Integrating with respect to position

If $a$ is known as a function of $s$, rewrite $a = v\,dv/ds$ as $v\,dv = a\,ds$ and integrate both sides:

$$\tfrac12 v^2 - \tfrac12 v(0)^2 = \int_{s(0)}^{s} a\,ds$$

This gives the speed at any position without ever finding $s(t)$. Multiply both sides by $m$ and you will recognize it later in the course as the work–energy principle: $\int ma\,ds$ is the work done by the net force.

Kinematics in the wild

A car’s speedometer shows v and the odometer shows s. The odometer is literally adding up the area under the speed curve. Crash investigators run the chain-rule formula backwards: from the length of the skid marks and the braking deceleration, ½v² = a·s gives the speed before braking.
