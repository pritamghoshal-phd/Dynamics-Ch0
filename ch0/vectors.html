AVector operations

A vector has a magnitude and a direction. In this course we write vectors in bold ($\mathbf a$) and describe them by their components along a set of perpendicular unit vectors (vectors of length one): $\hat{\mathbf i}, \hat{\mathbf j}, \hat{\mathbf k}$ along $x, y, z$:

$$\mathbf a = a_x\,\hat{\mathbf i} + a_y\,\hat{\mathbf j} + a_z\,\hat{\mathbf k}, \qquad |\mathbf a| = \sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}$$

Addition and subtraction

Geometrically, to add $\mathbf b$ to $\mathbf a$, place the tail of $\mathbf b$ at the head of $\mathbf a$; the sum runs from the tail of $\mathbf a$ to the head of $\mathbf b$. Doing it in the other order gives the same arrow, which is why the two paths form a parallelogram. Subtraction is addition of the reversed vector: $\mathbf a-\mathbf b = \mathbf a + (-\mathbf b)$.

With components, you simply add or subtract like components:

$$\mathbf a \pm \mathbf b = (a_x\pm b_x)\,\hat{\mathbf i} + (a_y\pm b_y)\,\hat{\mathbf j} + (a_z\pm b_z)\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$

The dot (scalar) product

The dot product of two vectors is a number:

$$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\cos\theta = a_xb_x + a_yb_y + a_zb_z$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them. The two forms agree because $\hat{\mathbf i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf i} = 1$ (parallel unit vectors) and $\hat{\mathbf i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf j} = 0$ (perpendicular unit vectors), so all the cross terms vanish. Two uses come up constantly:

  • Projection. If $\hat{\mathbf e}_a$ is the unit vector along $\mathbf a$, then $\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_a = |\mathbf b|\cos\theta$ is the component of $\mathbf b$ in the direction of $\mathbf a$.
  • Perpendicularity test. $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = 0$ (with neither vector zero) means they are perpendicular.

The cross (vector) product

The cross product of two vectors is another vector, perpendicular to both:

  • Magnitude: $|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b| = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\sin\theta$, which is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span.
  • Direction: by the right-hand rule. Point your fingers along $\mathbf a$, curl them toward $\mathbf b$, and your thumb points along $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$.
  • Order matters: $\mathbf b\times\mathbf a = -\,\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. Parallel vectors give zero.

For the unit vectors, $\hat{\mathbf i}\times\hat{\mathbf j}=\hat{\mathbf k}$, $\hat{\mathbf j}\times\hat{\mathbf k}=\hat{\mathbf i}$, $\hat{\mathbf k}\times\hat{\mathbf i}=\hat{\mathbf j}$ (cyclic order), and the reverse order flips the sign. In components, a determinant is the easiest bookkeeping:

$$\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\begin{vmatrix}\hat{\mathbf i}&\hat{\mathbf j}&\hat{\mathbf k}\\ a_x&a_y&a_z\\ b_x&b_y&b_z\end{vmatrix} = (a_yb_z-a_zb_y)\,\hat{\mathbf i}+(a_zb_x-a_xb_z)\,\hat{\mathbf j}+(a_xb_y-a_yb_x)\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$

Moment of a force

The most common use of the cross product in mechanics is the moment (torque) of a force $\mathbf F$ applied at a point P, about a point O:

$$\mathbf M_O = \mathbf r_{P/O}\times\mathbf F$$

where $\mathbf r_{P/O}$ points from O to P. Its magnitude is $|\mathbf F|\,d$, where $d$ is the perpendicular distance from O to the force's line of action (the lever arm). Sliding the force along its line of action does not change the moment; a force whose line passes through O has no moment about O.

ConceptMoment about O: r × F and the lever arm

Move P along the dashed line of action (change both x and y so that P stays on it) and the moment stays the same: only the lever arm d matters. Point F straight at O and the moment disappears.

Writing a vector in a different set of unit vectors

A vector is a physical arrow; its components depend on which axes you use. In dynamics we constantly switch between a fixed set $(\hat{\mathbf I},\hat{\mathbf J})$ and a set $(\hat{\mathbf i},\hat{\mathbf j})$ rotated by an angle $\theta$ (for example, axes attached to a turning body). The projection idea does all the work: a component is the dot product with a unit vector.

Reading the unit vectors off the figure,

$$\hat{\mathbf i} = \cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf I} + \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf J}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf j} = -\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf I} + \cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf J}$$

so for $\mathbf a = a_X\hat{\mathbf I} + a_Y\hat{\mathbf J}$,

$$a_x = \mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf i} = a_X\cos\theta + a_Y\sin\theta, \qquad a_y = \mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf j} = -a_X\sin\theta + a_Y\cos\theta$$

Tools in the wild

A long wrench makes loosening a stuck bolt easier because the moment is force times lever arm. Pull at the end of the handle, perpendicular to it, and the same effort gives the biggest moment. Pull along the handle and you get nothing at all: the line of action passes through the bolt.
