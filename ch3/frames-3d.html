B3D rotating reference frames

Good news first: the velocity and acceleration equations from Section A hold without change in three dimensions. The derivation is the same, with a third rotating unit vector $\hat k$ that also obeys $d\hat k/dt = \vec\omega\times\hat k$:

$$\vec v_B = \vec v_A + (\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} + \vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A}$$
$$\vec a_B = \vec a_A + (\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} + \vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A} + 2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$$

What is new is finding $\vec\omega$ and $\vec\alpha$ of the observer. In the plane, the observer always turned about the same fixed $\hat k$ axis. In 3D it usually turns about several axes at once, and some of those axes are themselves turning.

Angular velocity: add the rotations

If the observer's body spins at rate $\omega_1$ about one axis, while that axis is carried around at rate $\omega_2$ about another, the angular velocity is the vector sum

$$\vec\omega = \vec\omega_1 + \vec\omega_2 + \vec\omega_3 + \dots$$

Each component points along its own rotation axis (right-hand rule). Write all of them in one set of unit vectors before adding, usually the observer's $xyz$ at the instant of interest. For each component, note whether its axis is fixed in space or moves with some body: that decides the angular acceleration.

Angular acceleration: differentiate the direction too

By definition $\vec\alpha = d\vec\omega/dt$. Take a U-shaped frame turning at $\omega_1$ about the fixed vertical axis $\hat J$, carrying a disk that spins at $\omega_2$ about the frame's horizontal axis $\hat\imath$. Then $\vec\omega = \omega_1\hat J + \omega_2\hat\imath$, and the product rule gives

$$\vec\alpha = \dot\omega_1\hat J + \omega_1\underbrace{\frac{d\hat J}{dt}}_{=\,0\ \text{(fixed)}} + \dot\omega_2\hat\imath + \omega_2\underbrace{\frac{d\hat\imath}{dt}}_{=\,\omega_1\hat J\times\hat\imath}$$

With both rates constant, the first and third terms vanish but the last one does not: $\vec\alpha = \omega_1\omega_2\,(\hat J\times\hat\imath)$. The magnitude $|\vec\omega| = \sqrt{\omega_1^2+\omega_2^2}$ never changes, yet $\vec\alpha\neq\vec 0$, because the direction of $\vec\omega$ keeps turning. In 3D, "constant spin rates" does not mean "zero angular acceleration".

When differentiating a rotating unit vector, use the angular velocity of whatever body that unit vector is attached to. Here $\hat\imath$ is the axle of the U-frame, which turns at $\omega_1\hat J$ only, but using the disk's full $\vec\omega$ gives the same answer because $\omega_2\hat\imath\times\hat\imath = \vec 0$.

ConceptA spinning disk on a turning frame: ω swings around, so α ≠ 0

The red arrow is ω = ω₁Ĵ + ω₂î. Its tip runs around the grey circle at constant speed, and the green arrow, the velocity of that tip, is α. Set ω₁ = 0 or ω₂ = 0 and α vanishes.

3D rigid bodies

If B is a point of the observer's own body, the observer sees it standing still, so both "rel" terms are zero and

$$\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A}, \qquad \vec a_B = \vec a_A + \vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A} + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$$

These look exactly like the planar rigid-body equations, but now $\vec\omega$ can point in any direction, and $\vec\alpha$ need not be parallel to $\vec\omega$. For a body turning about a fixed point, every point moves on a circle around the $\vec\omega$ axis, with velocity perpendicular to both $\vec\omega$ and $\vec r$.

ConceptA block spinning about a tilted axis: v = ω × r at every point

The green arrows at the corners are ω × r. The dashed loop is the path of corner B: a circle in a plane perpendicular to ω. The statistics confirm v · ω = 0 and v · r = 0.

Checklist for 3D problems

  1. Attach the observer to the body you know most about (often the one whose angles you are given).
  2. List every rotation the observer takes part in, with its axis. Mark each axis as fixed or moving.
  3. Write $\vec\omega$ as a sum, all in the observer's $xyz$ unit vectors at the instant shown.
  4. Differentiate for $\vec\alpha$: rate changes give $\dot\omega_i$ terms; moving axes give $\omega_i\,(\vec\Omega\times\hat e_i)$ terms, with $\vec\Omega$ the angular velocity of the body carrying that axis.
  5. Describe what the observer sees, then substitute into the velocity and acceleration equations.

3D frames in the wild

A gyroscope resists being tilted because tilting its spin axis requires an angular acceleration α = ω₁ × ω₂, exactly the term in the disk demo. Spacecraft use spinning wheels and this effect to turn without firing thrusters.
