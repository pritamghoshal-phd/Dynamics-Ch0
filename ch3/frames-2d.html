A2D rotating reference frames

Place an observer at point A on a moving body and give the observer a set of axes $xyz$ that translate and rotate with the body. The fixed axes are $XYZ$. The observer describes the position of a point B as

$$\vec r_{B/A} = x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath$$

The catch is that $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ are not constant vectors. Their length is always one, but their direction turns with the observer. Before we can differentiate $\vec r_{B/A}$ we need to know how fast these unit vectors change.

The derivative of a rotating unit vector

Let the observer's axes be turned by an angle $\theta(t)$ from the fixed axes. The angular velocity of the observer is $\vec\omega = \dot\theta\,\hat k$. Writing the rotating unit vectors in fixed components,

$$\hat\imath = \cos\theta\,\hat I + \sin\theta\,\hat J, \qquad \hat\jmath = -\sin\theta\,\hat I + \cos\theta\,\hat J$$

and differentiating with the chain rule gives $d\hat\imath/dt = \dot\theta\,\hat\jmath$ and $d\hat\jmath/dt = -\dot\theta\,\hat\imath$. Both are captured by one compact rule:

$$\frac{d\hat\imath}{dt} = \vec\omega\times\hat\imath, \qquad \frac{d\hat\jmath}{dt} = \vec\omega\times\hat\jmath$$

The tip of a unit vector moves on a circle of radius one, so its velocity is perpendicular to the vector itself and has size $|\dot\theta|$.

The velocity equation

Start from $\vec r_B = \vec r_A + x\hat\imath + y\hat\jmath$ and differentiate, using the product rule on each term:

$$\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \underbrace{\dot x\,\hat\imath + \dot y\,\hat\jmath}_{(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel}} + \underbrace{x\,\vec\omega\times\hat\imath + y\,\vec\omega\times\hat\jmath}_{\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A}}$$

The first group is what the observer measures: the rate of change of B's coordinates in the observer's own axes, called the relative velocity. The second group is the extra velocity B gets simply because the observer's axes are swinging it around. Even a point that sits still in the observer's frame moves in the fixed frame.

ConceptOne motion, two observers: a bead sliding on a rotating rod

The rider on the rod sees the bead slide straight out with velocity (v)rel. The fixed observer sees a spiral. Head-to-tail, (v)rel plus ω × r gives the fixed-frame velocity v, and the last statistic checks it against the slope of the spiral path.

The acceleration equation

Differentiating the velocity equation once more, and again remembering that $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ turn, gives five terms:

$$\vec a_B = \vec a_A + (\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} + \vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A} + 2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$$
TermWhat it is
$\vec a_A$acceleration of the observer's origin A (fixed-frame)
$(\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} = \ddot x\,\hat\imath + \ddot y\,\hat\jmath$acceleration of B as measured by the moving observer
$\vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A}$because the observer's spin is speeding up or slowing down ($\vec\alpha = \dot\omega\,\hat k$)
$2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel}$the Coriolis term: appears only when B moves relative to a rotating observer
$\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$centripetal term, magnitude $\omega^2 r$, pointing from B toward A

Where does the factor of 2 in the Coriolis term come from? Half of it comes from the relative velocity changing direction as the axes turn ($\dot x\, d\hat\imath/dt$ terms); the other half comes from $\vec\omega\times\vec r$ growing because $\vec r$ changes length ($\vec\omega\times(\vec v)_{rel}$). Two separate effects, both of size $\omega\,v_{rel}$, in the same direction.

Coriolis, felt from the inside

The same equation read backwards tells a rotating observer what they will see. Solve for the relative acceleration:

$$(\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} = \vec a_B - \vec a_A - \vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A} - 2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} - \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$$

A puck sliding without friction has $\vec a_B = 0$, yet a rider on a turning platform sees it curve. The last two terms are the "Coriolis" and "centrifugal" effects the rider would describe as mysterious sideways and outward pushes. There is no extra force: the rider's axes are turning underneath the puck.

ConceptA straight path seen from a merry-go-round

Left: from the ground the puck travels in a straight line at constant speed. Right: the rider sees it curve away, against the direction of rotation, exactly as −2ω × (v)rel − ω × (ω × r) predicts.

When the point is on the same body

If B is fixed in the observer's body, the observer sees no motion: $(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} = (\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} = \vec 0$. The equations collapse to the rigid-body equations of Chapter 2. Conversely, if the observer only translates ($\vec\omega = \vec\alpha = \vec 0$), relative and absolute differences are the same thing. All the new content of this chapter lives in the rotation of the observer.

How to use the moving-frame equations

  1. Choose the observer and the body it rides on. Point A, its origin, must be a point on that body (often a fixed pivot).
  2. Draw the $xyz$ axes attached to that body, and the fixed $XYZ$ axes.
  3. Find $\vec\omega$ and $\vec\alpha$ of the observer. This is the motion of the observer, not what the observer sees.
  4. Imagine you are the observer. How does B move as you see it? Write $\vec r_{B/A}$, $(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel}$ and $(\vec a_{B/A})_{rel}$ in your own axes. For a pin in a straight slot, this motion is along the slot.
  5. Substitute into the velocity and acceleration equations. In mechanisms, also write an ordinary rigid-body equation for the other link that carries B, and equate the two expressions to solve for the unknowns.

Try this

  • In the bead demo, set the bead speed to 0. Which arrow disappears? What path does the bead trace in the fixed frame?
  • Make ω negative. Which way does the Coriolis arrow point now?
  • On the merry-go-round, double the platform rate. Does the curved path in the rider’s view bend more or less?
  • Compare $(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel}$ with $\vec v_{B/A} = \vec v_B - \vec v_A$. When are they equal? (Hint: the observer must not rotate.)
🌀
Coriolis in the wild

Earth is a rotating reference frame. Air flowing toward a low-pressure region is deflected sideways by the Coriolis effect, which is why hurricanes spin counterclockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern. Long-range artillery tables include a Coriolis correction for the same reason. Section C shows these effects, and several others, in animations.
