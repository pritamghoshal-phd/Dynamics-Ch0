CCoriolis in real life

The Coriolis term $2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel}$ can look like a piece of algebra that falls out of a derivative. This section shows that it describes real things you can see and measure. In every animation the motion comes from the true equations. Bodies that feel no horizontal force move in straight lines in the fixed frame, and everything else is found by integrating Newton's second law.

All of the examples use one idea. Solve the acceleration equation of Section A for what an observer on the turning body measures. With the observer's origin fixed and a constant rate $\omega$,

$$(\vec a_{B/A})_{rel} = \vec a_B - 2\vec\omega\times(\vec v_{B/A})_{rel} - \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_{B/A})$$

A rider on a merry-go-round, a person standing on the Earth and the controller of a robot all live in rotating frames. When they describe motion in their own frame, the last two terms show up as a sideways "Coriolis" push and an outward "centrifugal" push, even though no real force of that kind acts.

1. Playing catch on a merry-go-round

Two friends sit on opposite sides of a merry-go-round of radius 2 m. The thrower aims straight at the friend and throws. Once the ball leaves the hand, nothing pushes it sideways, so from the ground its path seen from above is a straight line. Two things spoil the throw. The ball starts with the thrower's own velocity $\vec\omega\times\vec r$ added to the throw, and the friend moves while the ball is in the air. The riders see the ball curve away from where they aimed. They can explain the curve exactly with the Coriolis and centrifugal accelerations drawn on the ball.

2. Where the factor of two comes from

Take a bead that slides outward along a turning rod at constant speed $u$. During a short time $\Delta t$ its velocity changes sideways in two separate ways, and each one is worth $\omega u$ per second. First, the relative velocity $u$ along the rod turns with the rod. Second, the velocity $\omega r$ that the bead gets from the rod's rotation grows, because $r$ grows. The two changes point the same way, so the sideways acceleration is $2\omega u$. A third change, $\omega r$ turning, points inward and is the centripetal term $\omega^2 r$.

3. The rotating Earth

The Earth turns once per sidereal day, $\Omega = 7.29\times10^{-5}$ rad/s. That is slow, but a long-range shell or a weather system moves fast enough, or for long enough, for the Coriolis acceleration to matter. At latitude $\lambda$ the Earth's spin has a vertical component $\Omega\sin\lambda$ and a horizontal component $\Omega\cos\lambda$ pointing north. For horizontal motion the vertical component is the important one. It is positive in the northern hemisphere and negative in the southern hemisphere, so moving bodies are deflected to the right in the north and to the left in the south.

The same deflection organizes the weather. Air flows toward a region of low pressure. In the northern hemisphere the moving air is turned to the right, so instead of flowing straight in it circles the low counterclockwise. In the southern hemisphere the sense reverses. Near the equator $\sin\lambda$ is close to zero, which is why hurricanes do not form there.

4. The Foucault pendulum

In 1851 Léon Foucault hung a 67 m pendulum in the Panthéon in Paris and showed the public that the Earth turns. A pendulum that is free to swing in any direction keeps swinging in the same plane in a non-rotating frame. The floor turns underneath it, so people standing on the floor see the plane of the swing turn. In the ground frame the explanation is the Coriolis acceleration $-2\Omega\sin\lambda\,\hat k\times\vec v$, which nudges the bob sideways on every swing. The plane turns at the rate $\Omega\sin\lambda$, clockwise in the northern hemisphere, once per sidereal day at the poles, and not at all at the equator.

5. Engineering: a robot arm that extends while it turns

A pick-and-place robot turns about a vertical axis and extends its arm at the same time. The gripper must supply the whole force that accelerates the payload. In polar components, with $r$ along the arm,

$$F_r = m(\ddot r - r\omega^2), \qquad F_\theta = m(r\alpha + 2\dot r\omega)$$

The term $2m\dot r\omega$ is the Coriolis force. It is needed only because the payload moves along an arm that turns. While an extending arm speeds up its turn, the motor must push much harder than the $mr^2\alpha$ term alone suggests. While it slows the turn, the Coriolis term works against the braking torque. A retracting arm behaves the other way round, the same effect that makes a figure skater spin faster when she pulls in her arms. Designers size motors and gearboxes for the peak torque, so ignoring this term gives a robot that cannot follow its own path.

🌍
Coriolis in the wild

Ballistic tables for long-range artillery and sniper rifles include Coriolis corrections. Mass-flow meters in chemical plants vibrate a bent tube and measure the tiny twist produced by the Coriolis force of the fluid flowing through it. The vibrating gyroscopes in every phone sense rotation through the Coriolis force on a vibrating mass.
