AKinematic equations for a rigid body

A rigid body is an object in which the distance between any two points never changes. In planar motion every point of the body moves in the same plane (or in parallel planes), so the body can slide around the plane and turn about an axis perpendicular to it, which we call $\hat{\mathbf k}$ (out of the page).

Angular velocity and angular acceleration

Pick two points A and B on the body. The vector $\mathbf r_{B/A}$ from A to B keeps a fixed length $r$, but its direction, measured by an angle $\theta$, changes as the body turns. Its rate of turning defines the angular velocity and angular acceleration of the body:

$$\boldsymbol\omega = \dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}, \qquad \boldsymbol\alpha = \ddot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$

By the right-hand rule, a positive value means counterclockwise (with $\hat{\mathbf k}$ out of the page) and a negative value means clockwise. Any other pair of points on the body, say D and E, gives a line whose angle differs from $\theta$ by a constant, so it turns at the same rate. That is why a rigid body has exactly one $\boldsymbol\omega$ and one $\boldsymbol\alpha$: they belong to the body, not to a particular pair of points.

The relative velocity equation

Write $\mathbf r_{B/A} = r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ in polar coordinates centered at A. From particle kinematics, $\mathbf v_{B/A} = \dot r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$. On a rigid body $\dot r = 0$, and since $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta = \hat{\mathbf k}\times\hat{\mathbf e}_r$,

$$\mathbf v_B = \mathbf v_A + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}$$

In words: the velocity of B is the velocity of A plus the velocity B would have if the body were spinning about A. The extra term $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}$ is always perpendicular to the line AB and has magnitude $|\omega|\,r$. This is the "rigid" condition: the two points can't approach or separate, so their relative velocity has no component along AB.

The relative acceleration equation

Doing the same with the polar acceleration formula, with $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$, gives

$$\mathbf a_B = \mathbf a_A + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_{B/A} + \boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}) = \mathbf a_A + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_{B/A} - \omega^2\,\mathbf r_{B/A}$$

(the last form holds for planar motion). The relative acceleration has two parts:

  • $\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_{B/A}$: tangential, perpendicular to AB, size $|\alpha| r$. It is there only if the body's spin is speeding up or slowing down.
  • $-\omega^2\mathbf r_{B/A}$: centripetal, pointing from B back toward A, size $\omega^2 r$. It is there whenever the body is turning.

So unlike the relative velocity, the relative acceleration is generally not perpendicular to AB.

Using the equations

  • $\mathbf r_{B/A}$ points from A to B, and A and B must be on the same rigid body.
  • Use axes with $\hat{\mathbf k}=\hat{\mathbf i}\times\hat{\mathbf j}$ (right-handed), so $\hat{\mathbf k}\times\hat{\mathbf i}=\hat{\mathbf j}$ and $\hat{\mathbf k}\times\hat{\mathbf j}=-\hat{\mathbf i}$.
  • If A is a fixed pin, $\mathbf v_A = \mathbf a_A = \mathbf 0$ and the body simply rotates about A: every point moves on a circle around A.
  • Each vector equation gives two scalar equations ($\hat{\mathbf i}$ and $\hat{\mathbf j}$ parts), so it can be solved for two unknowns.

Rolling without slipping

A wheel rolls on a fixed, rough surface. "No slip" means the point C of the wheel touching the ground has no velocity along the surface, and no acceleration along it either:

$$v_{Cx} = 0, \qquad a_{Cx} = 0$$

For a wheel of radius $R$ with center O, apply the rigid-body equations between O and C (with $\mathbf r_{C/O} = -R\,\hat{\mathbf j}$):

$$\mathbf v_C = (v_O + R\omega)\,\hat{\mathbf i} \;\Rightarrow\; v_O = -R\,\omega, \qquad \mathbf a_C = (a_O + R\alpha)\,\hat{\mathbf i} + R\omega^2\,\hat{\mathbf j} \;\Rightarrow\; a_O = -R\,\alpha$$

(the minus signs just say that rolling to the right means turning clockwise). Two surprises: the contact point has zero velocity but a non-zero acceleration, $R\omega^2$ straight up; and the top of the wheel moves at twice the speed of the center. If the wheel rolls on a body that is itself moving, "no slip" means the contact points share the same velocity and the same acceleration along the surface.
