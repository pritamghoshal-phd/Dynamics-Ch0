Example 2.A.7Link sliding on a wall and a floor

velocity equationacceleration equationconstrained ends

Problem

Link AB has length $L$. End A slides along a smooth vertical wall, and end B slides along a smooth horizontal floor. End B moves to the right with a constant speed $v_B$. The angle between the link and the floor is $\theta$.

The data are $v_B = 3$ m/s, $L = 0.5$ m and $\theta = 36.87°$.

Find (a) the angular velocity and (b) the angular acceleration of link AB. Express both answers as vectors.

Solution

Try the problem first, and then open one step at a time to check your work.

Step 1 · Draw a kinematics diagram

Take $\hat{\mathbf i}$ to the right, $\hat{\mathbf j}$ upward and $\hat{\mathbf k}$ out of the page. With $\cos\theta = 0.8$ and $\sin\theta = 0.6$, the position of A relative to B is

$$\mathbf r_{A/B} = -L\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf i} + L\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf j} = -0.4\,\hat{\mathbf i} + 0.3\,\hat{\mathbf j}\ \text{m}$$

The wall and the floor constrain the ends. Point A can move only vertically and point B only horizontally, so $\mathbf v_A = v_A\,\hat{\mathbf j}$ and $\mathbf a_A = a_A\,\hat{\mathbf j}$. Point B moves at constant velocity, so $\mathbf v_B = 3\,\hat{\mathbf i}$ m/s and $\mathbf a_B = \mathbf 0$. The unknowns are $\omega$, $v_A$, $\alpha$ and $a_A$.

The wall allows A to move only vertically, and the floor allows B to move only horizontally. B moves at constant velocity, so $\mathbf a_B = \mathbf 0$. The unknown rotation $\omega$ is drawn counterclockwise (positive).

Step 2 · Write the velocity equation $$\mathbf v_A = \mathbf v_B + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{A/B}$$ $$v_A\,\hat{\mathbf j} = 3\,\hat{\mathbf i} + \omega\,\hat{\mathbf k}\times(-0.4\,\hat{\mathbf i} + 0.3\,\hat{\mathbf j}) = (3 - 0.3\,\omega)\,\hat{\mathbf i} - 0.4\,\omega\,\hat{\mathbf j}$$

The $\hat{\mathbf i}$ components give $0 = 3 - 0.3\,\omega$, so $\omega = 10$ rad/s. The $\hat{\mathbf j}$ components then give $v_A = -4$ m/s, which means that A moves down the wall.

As a check, the instant center of AB is where the horizontal line through A meets the vertical line through B. Its distance from B is 0.3 m, so $\omega = v_B/0.3 = 10$ rad/s.

Step 3 · Write the acceleration equation $$\mathbf a_A = \mathbf a_B + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_{A/B} - \omega^2\,\mathbf r_{A/B}$$ $$a_A\,\hat{\mathbf j} = \mathbf 0 + \alpha\,\hat{\mathbf k}\times(-0.4\,\hat{\mathbf i} + 0.3\,\hat{\mathbf j}) - (10)^2(-0.4\,\hat{\mathbf i} + 0.3\,\hat{\mathbf j})$$ $$a_A\,\hat{\mathbf j} = (40 - 0.3\,\alpha)\,\hat{\mathbf i} + (-0.4\,\alpha - 30)\,\hat{\mathbf j}$$
Step 4 · Solve

The $\hat{\mathbf i}$ components give $0 = 40 - 0.3\,\alpha$, so $\alpha = 133.3$ rad/s². The $\hat{\mathbf j}$ components give $a_A = -0.4(133.3) - 30 = -83.3$ m/s².

The angular acceleration is large even though B moves at a constant speed. In general $\omega = v_B/(L\sin\theta)$, which grows quickly as $\theta$ becomes small, and the plots in the animation show this growth.

Answer

(a) $\boldsymbol\omega_{AB} = 10\,\hat{\mathbf k}$ rad/s (counterclockwise). (b) $\boldsymbol\alpha_{AB} = 133.3\,\hat{\mathbf k}$ rad/s² (counterclockwise).

  • Halve $L$ with the slider. By what factors do $\omega$ and $\alpha$ change at the same angle?
  • Why can the instant center give $\omega$ but not $\alpha$?
