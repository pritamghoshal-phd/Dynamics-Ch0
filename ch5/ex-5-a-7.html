Example 5.A.7A ladder in an accelerating cart

Newton–Eulerfrictionimpending slip

Problem

A ladder rests against the rough vertical wall and the rough floor of a cart. A person stands on the ladder so that the mass center of the person and ladder together is at G, a height $h$ above the floor and a horizontal distance $d$ from the wall. The combined weight is $W$. The cart accelerates to the left with acceleration $a$, toward the side with the wall.

Find the smallest coefficient of static friction $\mu_s$, the same at both ends of the ladder, for which the ladder does not slip.

Use $W = 250$ lb, $\theta = 70°$, $L = 15$ ft, $h = 10$ ft, $d = 5$ ft and $a = 5$ ft/s².

If $\mu_s \ge \mu_{s,\min}$, the ladder stays put and the drawn forces are the limiting values. If $\mu_s$ is smaller, both ends slide: the foot moves away from the wall and the top slides down until it leaves the wall. With the book value $d = 5$ ft, the wall would have to pull on the ladder, so the top leaves the wall at once and the ladder tips backward about its foot A.

Solution

Every solution follows the four-step method. Try the problem first, and then open one step at a time to check your work.

Step 1 · Draw the free-body diagram

The ladder and person are acted on by the weight $W$ at G, by a normal force $N_A$ and a horizontal friction force $F_A$ at the foot A, and by a horizontal normal force $N_B$ and a vertical friction force $F_B$ at the top B. The wall can only push on the ladder, so $N_B \ge 0$. We place the origin at the bottom of the wall, with $x$ to the right and $y$ upward. Then A is at $(x_A, 0)$ with $x_A = L\cos\theta = 5.13$ ft, B is at $(0, y_B)$ with $y_B = L\sin\theta = 14.10$ ft, and G is at $(d, h)$.

The forces $F_A$ and $F_B$ are drawn in the positive $x$ and $y$ directions. In the limiting case found below, $F_A$ points toward the wall and $F_B$ points up.

Step 2 · Write the kinetics equations

If the ladder does not slip, it moves with the cart, so $\mathbf a_G = -a\,\hat{\mathbf i}$ and $\alpha = 0$. Newton's equation gives

$$F_A + N_B = -\frac Wg\,a, \qquad N_A + F_B - W = 0$$

For a translating body, the moment about any point A equals $\mathbf r_{G/A}\times m\mathbf a_G$. Taking moments about the foot A removes $N_A$ and $F_A$.

$$-y_B N_B - x_A F_B + (x_A - d)\,W = \frac Wg\,a\,h$$
Step 3 · Write the friction conditions

There are four unknowns and three equations, so we use the condition of impending slip. A rigid ladder can slip in only two ways. Either the foot A slides away from the wall while B slides down, or A slides toward the wall while B slides up. In the first case $F_A = -\mu_s N_A$ and $F_B = +\mu_s N_B$, and in the second case the signs are reversed.

Step 4 · Solve

With the book data. Ignoring $F_B$ for a moment, the moment equation gives

$$N_B \approx \frac{(x_A - d)W - (W/g)\,a\,h}{y_B} = \frac{(5.13 - 5)(250) - (7.76)(5)(10)}{14.10} = -25\ \text{lb}$$

The wall would have to pull on the ladder. The mass center is almost directly above the foot A, so its weight barely presses the ladder toward the wall, while the acceleration tends to tip it away. Solving the full equations with either friction pattern confirms that no physically consistent solution exists. Equilibrium would require $\mu_s \ge \tan 70° = 2.75$, and even then the friction forces would grow without bound. With these numbers the ladder tips away from the wall.

With G beside the ladder. In the figure in the book, G is drawn just beside the ladder, about $d = 1.5$ ft from the wall at the height $h = 10$ ft. With that value the first friction pattern applies, and the three equations give

$$N_A = 240.0\ \text{lb}, \quad F_A = -72.0\ \text{lb}, \quad N_B = 33.2\ \text{lb}, \quad F_B = 10.0\ \text{lb}, \quad \mu_s = 0.300$$
Answer

With the stated value $d = 5$ ft, the wall would have to pull on the ladder ($N_B \approx -25$ lb), so no realistic friction coefficient can hold it and the ladder tips away from the wall. With G placed beside the ladder as drawn ($d \approx 1.5$ ft), the required coefficient is $\mu_{s,min} \approx 0.30$.

  • With $d = 1.5$ ft, increase the acceleration. Does the required friction coefficient rise or fall?
