AThe Newton–Euler equations

A rigid body can be thought of as a very large number of particles that cannot move relative to one another. If we write Newton's second law for every particle and add the results, the internal forces cancel in pairs. Two equations remain, and together they describe the motion of the body.

Newton's equation describes the motion of the mass center G:

$$\sum\mathbf F = m\,\mathbf a_G$$

Euler's equation describes the rotation. For planar motion, with moments taken about any point A of the body, it reads

$$\sum\mathbf M_A = I_A\,\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf r_{G/A}\times m\,\mathbf a_A$$

Here $I_A$ is the mass moment of inertia about an axis through A, $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is the angular acceleration of the body, and $\mathbf r_{G/A}$ is the position vector from A to G.

Two equations with two separate jobs

Newton's equation states that G moves exactly like a particle of mass $m$ acted on by the total force. This is true no matter where on the body the forces are applied. Euler's equation, written about G, becomes $\sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha$. It states that the body rotates as if it were pinned at G and acted on by the total moment. The location of a force therefore changes the rotation of the body, but it never changes the motion of G.

Choosing the moment point

Moments may be taken about any point. The last term in Euler's equation vanishes in three convenient cases, and these are the points you should normally use.

  • If A is the mass center G, the equation becomes $\sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha$.
  • If A is a fixed pivot O, the equation becomes $\sum M_O = I_O\,\alpha$. The pin reactions at O pass through O, so they do not appear in this equation.
  • If the acceleration of A points along the line from A to G, the cross product is zero. The most common example is the contact point C of a uniform wheel that rolls without slipping. The acceleration of C points toward the center of the wheel, which is G, so $\sum M_C = I_C\,\alpha$.

Whichever point you choose, use it consistently for the moments, for $I_A$, for $\mathbf a_A$ and for $\mathbf r_{G/A}$. In Newton's equation the acceleration is always that of G. Use the same sign convention for moments and for $\alpha$. The usual choice is counterclockwise positive, with $\hat{\mathbf k}$ pointing out of the page.

The mass moment of inertia

The moment of inertia measures how the mass of a body is distributed about an axis. It is defined as $I_A = \int r^2\,dm$, where $r$ is the distance of the mass element $dm$ from the axis through A. Because the distance is squared, mass far from the axis contributes much more than mass close to it. The animation below makes this concrete. Two rotors carry the same masses and are driven by the same moment, but the masses sit at different radii.

Two results allow us to move these values to other axes. The parallel-axis theorem states that $I_A = I_G + m\,d^2$, where $d$ is the distance between the axis through G and the parallel axis through A. Since $md^2$ can never be negative, the moment of inertia of a body is smallest about its mass center. The radius of gyration is defined by $k_A = \sqrt{I_A/m}$, so that $I_A = m k_A^2$. It is the distance from the axis at which all of the mass could be concentrated without changing $I_A$.

Forces at a pin

A pin does more than hold a body up. Because G moves on a circle about the pin, the pin must supply whatever force is needed to give G its tangential acceleration $r\alpha$ and its normal acceleration $r\omega^2$. For a uniform bar of length $L$ that is pinned at one end and released from rest in a horizontal position, Newton's equation in the normal and tangential directions gives

$$O_n = \tfrac52\,mg\sin\theta, \qquad O_t = \tfrac14\,mg\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle of the bar below the horizontal, $O_n$ pulls G toward the pin and $O_t$ acts perpendicular to the bar. At the instant of release the pin carries only one quarter of the weight, because the bar is starting to fall. As the bar passes through the vertical position the pin must pull upward with $2.5\,mg$.

Translation without rotation: sliding or tipping?

When a body translates without rotating, $\alpha = 0$, and Euler's equation about G becomes $\sum M_G = 0$. This condition decides how the normal force is shared between the supports. Consider a crate that is pushed across a rough floor. The push and the friction force both try to rotate the crate forward, so the front edge must carry more of the weight than the rear edge. If the push is applied high enough, the required rear normal force becomes negative. A floor cannot pull on the crate, so the crate tips about its front edge instead of sliding.

Rolling down a slope

Consider a round body of radius $R$ and radius of gyration $k_G$ that rolls without slipping down an incline of angle $\theta$. Its mass center accelerates at

$$a_G = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k_G^2/R^2}$$

This result does not depend on the mass or on the size of the body. Only the shape of the mass distribution, expressed by the ratio $k_G/R$, decides which body is fastest.

ConceptA rolling race between a hoop, a disk, a sphere and a sliding block

Every body starts from rest at the top of an identical slope. The frictionless block does not rotate, so all of the work done by gravity goes into translation, and the block wins. Among the rolling bodies, the one with the least mass far from its center is fastest.

Which way does a pulled spool roll?

The contact point is often the best moment point for a rolling body, because $\sum M_C = I_C\,\alpha$ and the unknown friction and normal forces drop out. A spool that is pulled by a string wound around its hub shows how useful this is. Only the string tension has a moment about C. If its line of action passes above C, the spool rolls toward the pull. If it passes below C, the spool rolls away from the pull, even though the string is pulling it forward. The two cases are separated by the critical angle $\cos\varphi_c = r/R$, at which the line of action passes through C and the spool does not roll at all.

From skidding to rolling

A bowling ball is usually released with a forward speed $v_0$ and little spin, so its contact point slides along the lane. Kinetic friction then acts against the sliding. Newton's equation shows that friction slows the ball, $\dot v = -\mu_k g$, while Euler's equation about G shows that the same force spins it up, $R\dot\omega = \tfrac52\mu_k g$ for a solid sphere. When $v = R\omega$, the contact point stops sliding, friction vanishes, and the ball rolls at a constant speed. With no initial spin the final speed is $\tfrac57 v_0$, whatever the friction coefficient.

🛹
Rigid bodies in the wild

Figure skaters, divers and falling cats all control their rotation by changing their moment of inertia. A hammer is also easier to swing when you hold it near the head, because a pivot closer to G gives a smaller moment of inertia about your hand.
