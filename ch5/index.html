Chapter 5 · Planar Rigid Body Kinetics

In Chapter 4 every body was treated as a single point. A rigid body has size and shape, so it can rotate as well as translate, and the point where a force is applied now matters. This chapter extends the three tools developed for particles (Newton's second law, the work–energy principle and the impulse–momentum principle) to rigid bodies that move in a plane. The four-step method for kinetics

  1. Draw the free-body diagrams. Decide which bodies, or which system of bodies, to isolate. Then draw every external force and couple that acts on each one, together with the coordinate directions you intend to use.
  2. Write the kinetics equations. Choose a method (Newton–Euler, work–energy or impulse–momentum) and write its equations using the forces on your diagrams.
  3. Write the kinematics equations. Relate the accelerations or velocities that appear in Step 2. Typical relations come from rolling without slipping, from cables, and from the rigid-body velocity and acceleration equations of Chapter 2.
  4. Solve. Count the unknowns and the equations. If there are fewer equations than unknowns, you need another free-body diagram or more kinematics.

Which method should I use?

All three methods are correct for every problem, but one of them is usually much shorter. The table below gives a good first guess.

If the question asks forthe easiest method is usually
an acceleration, an angular acceleration or a force at one instantNewton–Euler (Section A)
a speed after the body has moved through a distance or an anglework–energy (Section B)
a velocity after a known time, or immediately after an impactimpulse–momentum (Section C)
