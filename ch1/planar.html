APlanar kinematics: Cartesian, path and polar

A particle P moves in a plane. Its position vector $\mathbf r$ points from a fixed origin O to P. Its velocity and acceleration are the time derivatives of that vector:

$$\mathbf v = \frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}, \qquad \mathbf a = \frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}$$

The velocity is always tangent to the path. The acceleration generally is not: it points partly along the path (changing the speed) and partly toward the inside of the curve (changing the direction).

1. Cartesian description

Write $\mathbf r = x\,\hat{\mathbf i} + y\,\hat{\mathbf j}$. Because $\hat{\mathbf i}$ and $\hat{\mathbf j}$ are fixed in direction, differentiating only touches the components:

$$\mathbf v = \dot x\,\hat{\mathbf i} + \dot y\,\hat{\mathbf j}, \qquad \mathbf a = \ddot x\,\hat{\mathbf i} + \ddot y\,\hat{\mathbf j}$$

The two directions are completely independent: the $x$ motion knows nothing about the $y$ motion. Projectile motion is the classic case, with $\ddot x = 0$ and $\ddot y = -g$.

2. Path description (tangential and normal)

Here the unit vectors ride along with the particle. $\hat{\mathbf e}_t$ is tangent to the path in the direction of motion, and $\hat{\mathbf e}_n$ is perpendicular to it, pointing toward the center of curvature (the inside of the bend). The velocity is simply

$$\mathbf v = v\,\hat{\mathbf e}_t$$

where $v$ is the speed. Differentiating, the speed can change and the direction $\hat{\mathbf e}_t$ can turn. A turning unit vector has derivative $\dot{\hat{\mathbf e}}_t = (v/\rho)\,\hat{\mathbf e}_n$, where $\rho$ is the radius of curvature of the path at that point. So

$$\mathbf a = \dot v\,\hat{\mathbf e}_t + \frac{v^2}{\rho}\,\hat{\mathbf e}_n$$
  • Tangential part $a_t = \dot v$: changes the speed. Zero for constant speed.
  • Normal part $a_n = v^2/\rho$: changes the direction. Zero on a straight path ($\rho\to\infty$). It always points to the inside of the curve, never outward.
ConceptA car on a winding road: v̇ and v²/ρ
Speed profile:

The purple dotted circle is the circle that best fits the road at the car (the osculating circle); its radius is ρ. Where the road is straight for an instant (inflection points), ρ becomes infinite and the normal acceleration vanishes. At constant speed the acceleration is purely normal.

3. Polar description

Locate P by its distance $r$ from O and the angle $\theta$ of the line OP. The unit vector $\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ points from O to P and $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ is perpendicular to it, in the direction of increasing $\theta$. Both turn with the line OP at rate $\dot\theta$:

$$\dot{\hat{\mathbf e}}_r = \dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta, \qquad \dot{\hat{\mathbf e}}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r$$

Starting from $\mathbf r = r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ and differentiating twice gives

$$\mathbf v = \dot r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$$ $$\mathbf a = \left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right)\hat{\mathbf e}_r + \left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right)\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$$

Two terms deserve names. $-r\dot\theta^2$ is the centripetal term: anything that goes around O must be pulled toward O. $2\dot r\dot\theta$ is the Coriolis term: if P moves outward along a turning line, the line must also push it sideways, because points farther out move faster.

Kinematics in the wild

Roller-coaster loops are not circles: they are tighter at the top than at the bottom (a "clothoid" shape). Because the normal acceleration is v²/ρ, a smaller ρ at the top, where the car is slowest, keeps riders pressed into their seats without crushing them at the bottom, where the car is fastest.
