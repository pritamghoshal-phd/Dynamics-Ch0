B3D kinematics: Cartesian and cylindrical

Nothing new is needed to move from the plane to space. We add a third unit vector and keep differentiating.

3D Cartesian description

With fixed unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf i}, \hat{\mathbf j}, \hat{\mathbf k}$,

$$\mathbf r = x\hat{\mathbf i} + y\hat{\mathbf j} + z\hat{\mathbf k}, \quad \mathbf v = \dot x\hat{\mathbf i} + \dot y\hat{\mathbf j} + \dot z\hat{\mathbf k}, \quad \mathbf a = \ddot x\hat{\mathbf i} + \ddot y\hat{\mathbf j} + \ddot z\hat{\mathbf k}$$

When the particle is confined to a surface, say $z = f(x, y)$, the third coordinate is not independent: differentiate the surface equation with the chain rule to get $\dot z$ and $\ddot z$ from the $x$ and $y$ motion.

The green dashed box shows x, y and z. Each component is differentiated on its own; the three results are then reassembled into v (orange) and a (red).

Cylindrical description

Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates plus height. P is located by its distance $r$ from the $z$-axis, the angle $\theta$ around it, and the height $z$. The unit vectors are $\hat{\mathbf e}_r$ and $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ (which turn, exactly as in polar) and $\hat{\mathbf k}$ (which does not):

$$\mathbf r = r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r + z\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$ $$\mathbf v = \dot r\,\hat{\mathbf e}_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta + \dot z\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$ $$\mathbf a = \left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right)\hat{\mathbf e}_r + \left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right)\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta + \ddot z\,\hat{\mathbf k}$$

In words: the shadow of P on the $xy$-plane moves exactly as in polar coordinates, and the height is handled separately, just like a Cartesian coordinate.

Kinematics in the wild

A screw thread, a spiral staircase and the path of a charged particle in a magnetic field are all helices. In each case the motion around the axis and the motion along it are separate, which is exactly what cylindrical coordinates capture.
